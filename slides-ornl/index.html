Quantum Computing and Circuit Simulation

Thesis statement

Useful quantum computation in the coming decade will be delivered not by a quantum processor alone but by a co-designed stack in which classical high-performance computing surrounds the QPU at every layer.

Introduction
This is the thesis in one sentence, taken verbatim from the dissertation. The word to underline is co-designed: HPC is not a stopgap until hardware improves, it is a permanent part of how quantum programs are developed, run and trusted. Each of the following sections adds one layer to that stack.

Introduction
Exact state-vector simulation is the reference tool for developing and verifying circuits, but its memory doubles per qubit. At 42 qubits the state alone is about seventy terabytes in double precision, far beyond any single node. The question for RQ1 is how far HPC can push this and whether a Qiskit program can use that capability without being rewritten.

Simulation · Q-GEAR
Layer one: simulation. Q-GEAR, published at ICPP 2025 with Jan Balewski from NERSC, is the framework that lets a Qiskit circuit run on NVIDIA GPUs at Perlmutter scale without a rewrite. This chapter answers RQ1.
Design

The kernel transformation pipeline

Simulation · Q-GEAR
End to end, the pipeline has six stages, and Q-GEAR automates the four in the middle. The two highlighted stages, encoding and kernel generation, are the contribution; packaging in Podman or Shifter containers is what makes the result reproducible on Perlmutter's Slurm scheduler.
Deployment

One node to a thousand GPUs

Simulation · Q-GEAR
Each Perlmutter GPU node has four 40-gigabyte A100s connected by NVLink, and CUDA-Q's multi-GPU backend shards the state vector across them. Beyond one node, MPI over the Slingshot interconnect lets us aggregate memory across nodes: 256 nodes give 1,024 GPUs, which is where the 42-qubit runs happened at close to full GPU utilization.

Algorithms · DEAL & QAWA
Layer two: algorithms on noisy hardware. Two papers: DEAL, published at IEEE QCE 2025, replaces black-box QAOA angle search with a direct mapping from the problem; QAWA proposes a data-traceable oracle so that QAOA outputs can be interpreted without tomography. Both answer RQ2.
DEAL · Direct Entanglement Ansatz Learning

Read the angles off the problem

Direct angle mapping

Angles from the QUBO coefficients — no optimizer loop

Entanglement-based ansatz

Echoed cross-resonance gates within the device’s CZ budget

Adaptive digital ZNE

Delay / identity gates vs crosstalk; Bayesian layer choice

Algorithms · DEAL & QAWA
DEAL has three parts. First, the angles are read directly off the QUBO coefficients, so there is no optimizer loop on hardware. Second, the entangling structure is chosen for the device, using echoed cross-resonance gates. Third, an adaptive digital zero-noise extrapolation cleans up the result. The circuits are twenty qubits and up to ten layers on the Heron processors Torino and Marrakesh.
Results · hardware

DEAL beats vanilla QAOA on Heron-1

20 qubits, 10 layers; success = reaching the ground-state energy. TSP, knapsack and MaxCut instances; lower variance too.

Algorithms · DEAL & QAWA
On IBM Torino, a 133-qubit Heron-1, DEAL reaches the ground-state energy 14.8 percentage points more often than vanilla QAOA at ten layers, with lower variance; on the right, seven DEAL layers match what QAOA needs nine for, which matters because each layer spends part of the forty-CZ budget. The Heron-2 repeat (Marrakesh, +7.4 points) is in the backup slides. Every one of these circuits was validated with the Q-GEAR GPU path before it went to hardware.

Data · Quantum learning
Layer three is the data interface, and it is the largest chapter: four papers. QPIE is a hybrid network published in Quantum Science and Technology; VQT is a quantum transformer from the AAAI Symposium Series; ShardQ cuts and knits encoding circuits; and NNQA compiles trained polynomial networks into exact quantum arithmetic. All four answer RQ3.
Building block

QCrank: vectorized data encoding

Address qubits index the values; data qubits carry them as angles. Shared by Q-GEAR, QPIE, VQT, ShardQ, QAWA. A 1,000-pixel image: 9 + 2 qubits.

Data · Quantum learning
QCrank, due to Balewski and colleagues at Berkeley Lab, is the encoding this chapter builds on. Address qubits in uniform superposition index the values; each data qubit carries two-to-the-n-a values as rotation angles through a uniformly controlled rotation. The circuit is linear in the number of values and processes all addresses in parallel, which is what makes vectorized arithmetic possible. A thousand-pixel image needs nine address and two data qubits.

Data · Quantum learning
ShardQ makes the loader hardware-compatible. The deterministic SparseCut algorithm chooses gate cuts using physical qubit distance and calibrated error rates; sub-circuits are compiled with a bounded-bond-dimension MPS compiler; and the outputs are knitted back with a global quasi-probability reconstruction rather than pairwise. On Heron this cuts RMSE by about fifteen percent and lifts image-encoding fidelity from 0.79 to 0.90. The knitting itself is HPC work: two cuts take fifteen seconds on one A100, four cuts a day on four GPU nodes.
NNQA · Neural-Native Quantum Arithmetic

Compile, do not search

>99.5% accuracy up to degree 35
36 qubits · depth 70 (IonQ Forte-1 stress test)

pass rates 74–86% Heron-3 · 92–99% Forte-1

Data · Quantum learning
NNQA closes the trainability corner by not training on the QPU at all. A polynomial neural network is trained classically on Perlmutter, then compiled exactly into native unitary blocks for weighted sums and products on real-valued encoded states. A degree-6 polynomial needs seven qubits, five CNOTs and depth nineteen, run once, where VQE needs hundreds of executions. On Heron-3, Nighthawk and IonQ Forte-1 it holds above 99.5 percent accuracy up to degree 35, and we prove a universal-approximation theorem for the construction.
Takeaway

Vectorize, then learn

  • One encoding, four uses: QPIE blocks · VQT attention · ShardQ cuts · NNQA arithmetic
  • Shot-efficient: VQDP O(log(B·T²)) vs O(B·T²·d) · NNQA runs once
  • Hardware-compatible: fidelity 0.79 → 0.90 · ran on Heron, Aria-1, Forte-1
  • Trainable: learnable heads keep gradients alive · NNQA needs no on-device training

Synthesis & cost
Layer four: synthesis and cost. RubriQ uses a language model fine-tuned by reinforcement learning, with a programmatic rubric as the reward, to synthesize circuits toward fault-tolerant resource budgets. The second paper is the honest-accounting half of RQ4: what nonlinear-wave simulation really costs when every step must read the field.
RubriQ · Rubric-guided GRPO

Synthesis as code generation

Synthesis & cost
RubriQ frames fault-tolerant synthesis as code generation. A seven-billion-parameter coder with LoRA adapters proposes circuits; they are parsed, simulated with CUDA-Q on the GPU, scored by a rubric, and the policy is updated with Group Relative Policy Optimization, which compares eight samples against each other instead of learning a value network. Training runs on Perlmutter with DeepSpeed across up to eight A100 nodes at 85 percent strong-scaling efficiency.

Synthesis & cost
On 1,500 benchmark circuits spanning QFT, phase estimation, Hamiltonian simulation and data encoding, RubriQ compresses T-gate counts by 3.31 times on average against 2.05 for sparse-reward RL, keeps hardware-constraint violations under one percent, and reports 96 percent correctness. The synthesized circuits were validated on Heron-3 and IonQ Forte, so this is not only a simulator result.
Nonlinear waves · Cost model

Where the quantum step loses

Schematic from the cost model: depth Θ(N) × shots S(N) per step vs O(N·d) classical, normalized at N = 32. Not measured wall time.

Synthesis & cost
The conclusion is negative but quantitative. Because every step reads the full field, the quantum cost per step, depth times shots, grows faster than the classical polynomial update as N grows, so the hybrid scheme cannot deliver an end-to-end advantage. What it does deliver is the target: a coherent, measurement-free nonlinear update is what any future advantage claim must beat. This chart is the schematic scaling from the model, not measured wall time.
Takeaway

Honest accounting

  • Grounded reward works: rubric + GPU simulation → 3.31× T-gate compression, <1% violations
  • HPC inside the loop: CUDA-Q evaluation (Q-GEAR path) + DeepSpeed on Perlmutter
  • Count everything: measurement + reload every step → no end-to-end advantage for nonlinear waves
  • Both stated as budgets: T-gates · shots × depth

Trust · QEC audit
Layer five: trust. This chapter, with coauthors from Lightrider and Quantropi, treats the error-correction encoder on a shared cloud QPU as a security surface and asks how randomness should be used and audited. It is the first half of the answer to RQ5.
Threat model

A public encoder is a reusable target

Trust · QEC audit
The setting is a shared cloud processor: your error-corrected job runs next to a co-tenant who can inject a single Pauli fault. If the encoder circuit is fixed and public, the adversary can learn exactly which physical fault produces a harmful logical action that the syndrome check will not catch, and reuse it every run. If the encoder is reseeded per run, the fault has to be chosen before the seed is known. The fault model is deliberately bounded: weight-one Paulis, all three-n locations enumerated.

Trust · QEC audit
With the five-qubit code and every weight-one fault enumerated, a fixed public encoder gives a mean accepted logical disturbance of 0.150. Reseeding the encoder per run brings it to 0.020, an 86.7 percent reduction, and the reduction is attributable to rejection: with a fresh encoder the pre-chosen fault is caught by the syndrome check most of the time. Gate-level Stim simulations at 17 qubits confirm the trend. The seed is the only secret, which is where the post-quantum module in the backup slides comes in.

Limitations

What these results do not show

  • 42 qubits is simulation, not hardware — largest hardware runs: 20-qubit optimization, 36-qubit arithmetic
  • Hardware gains are device- and calibration-specific — +14.81 Torino vs +7.40 Marrakesh
  • Learning benchmarks are small — VQT 12.9 perplexity behind nanoGPT; no advantage claimed
  • QEC audit at code-capacity scale — [[5,1,3]], weight-one faults, Stim n = 17
  • Six of ten papers are preprints — numbers may change in review
Closing
I want to be explicit about limits. The 42-qubit result is simulation: Q-GEAR's scaling is a statement about HPC reach, and the largest hardware runs are 20-qubit optimization circuits and 36-qubit arithmetic stress tests. The hardware gains depend on device and calibration cycle: DEAL gains 14.81 points on Torino but 7.40 on Marrakesh, VQT's RMSE ranges from 0.059 on Kingston to 0.155 on Aria-1, and repeats across calibration cycles are limited. The learning benchmarks are small, Moon, Spiral, MNIST and the Brown corpus, and VQT remains 12.9 perplexity points behind a 125K-parameter nanoGPT. The audit covers the five-qubit code with weight-one faults and Stim at 17 qubits; surface-code distances and correlated faults are future work. And six of the ten papers, ShardQ, QAWA, NNQA, RubriQ, nonlinear waves and the QEC audit, are still preprints. The hardware gains depend on which device and which calibration cycle we hit. The learning benchmarks are small and I claim no advantage there. The security audit is at five-qubit-code scale. And six of the ten papers are still preprints. These are the boundaries within which the answers hold.
